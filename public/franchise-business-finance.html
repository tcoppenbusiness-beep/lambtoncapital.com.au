How much can I borrow to buy a franchise?

Often between 50% and 70% of the purchase price, lender-set and indicative, depending on the brand and the security you offer.

  • The rest. Comes from your own cash, a vendor loan or another asset.
  • Brand strength. A well-known brand with a long record sits toward the top of that range. A new or small brand sits toward the bottom.
  • Goodwill. Most of what you buy is not physical: the right to trade under the brand, the territory and the customers. Lenders call that goodwill (the value of a business beyond its physical assets) and lend less against it, because there is less to sell if things go wrong.

Why does franchise accreditation matter to lenders?

Some lenders keep a list of franchise systems they have already reviewed, and buyers in those systems often get better terms and a smaller deposit.

  • Accreditation. This is sometimes called franchise accreditation. The lender has already checked the franchisor's contracts, training and failure record.
  • Not on the list. The brand can still be financed, but the lender assesses the franchisor from scratch.
  • What to ask. Ask the franchisor which lenders already know the system, then ask us which of those suits you.

What do the franchisor and the landlord need to approve?

Both usually need to sign off before the loan can settle (the loan funds are paid and the deal completes).

  • Franchisor. Must approve you as the new owner. Lenders make that approval a condition of the loan.
  • Landlord. Must agree to assign (transfer) the lease to you. Lenders want to see the assigned lease before they release funds.
  • Allow weeks. Start both approvals alongside the loan application, not after, and check the contract for a deadline.

Can the seller help fund the purchase?

Yes, through vendor finance (the seller lets you pay part of the price later) or an earn-out (part of the price is paid later, depending on future profits).

  • Vendor loan. A 10–20% vendor loan cuts the cash you need on day one and shows the lender the seller believes in the numbers.
  • Lender conditions. The vendor loan must rank behind the lender's, and payments to the seller are often limited until the main loan is under way.
  • Earn-out. Similar benefit, but more complex to document.
  • Franchisor limits. Franchisors sometimes restrict both, so read the franchise agreement first.

Example structure — illustrative

Franchise cafe, Bendigo VIC

Situation: A buyer with a hospitality background taking over an established coffee franchise from a retiring owner, with five years left on the lease.

Constraint: Goodwill made up most of the price, and the buyer had limited cash and no property to offer.

Structure: Loan from a lender that has approved the franchise brand, repaid over 7 years and secured by the business assets. The buyer's family offered a second-ranking mortgage over a home for part of the shortfall, and the seller provided a 10% vendor loan.

What lenders ask for

  • The franchise agreement and the franchisor's written approval, or the process to obtain it
  • The sale contract with price breakdown (goodwill, stock, equipment, lease)
  • Three years of financials and tax returns for the outlet, plus current trading figures
  • Your own financials, a statement of assets and debts, and proof of the cash you are contributing
  • The lease and the landlord's agreement to assign it to you
  • A plan for the first 12 months, including how you will cover wages and rent

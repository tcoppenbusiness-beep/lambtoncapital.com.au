How does truck and machinery finance work?

The lender pays for the machine and holds it as security (an asset they can claim if the loan isn't repaid) until you repay.

  • Chattel mortgage. The most common form (a loan to buy equipment, secured by that item). You own the machine from day one, and the lender's claim ends with the last payment.
  • Machine checks. Lenders look hard at make, model, age, hours or kilometres, and how easily it resells.
  • Resale matters. A late-model Isuzu or Hino tipper is easy to resell. A heavily modified special-purpose rig is not, and lenders price that in.

Does it matter if the truck or excavator is new or used?

Yes. New equipment from a dealer gets the widest choice of lenders and the longest terms.

  • Used is financed too. Lenders set age limits.
  • Age plus term. A common rule, lender-set and indicative: machine age plus loan term should not exceed a set number of years, often 15 to 20 for trucks and excavators.
  • Example. A seven-year-old excavator may only be financed over three or four years, with higher repayments.
  • Hours count. An excavator with 9,000 hours is treated more cautiously than one with 3,000.

What changes if I buy from a private seller instead of a dealer?

The lender does more checking, because a dealer is a business with obligations and a private seller is not.

  • PPSR search. A search of the Personal Property Securities Register, which shows whether anyone else has a claim over the machine.
  • Existing claims. If another lender has a claim, the machine cannot be sold clear until it is paid out at settlement.
  • Extra checks. Lenders may ask for a valuation or inspection report on older machines, and some will not fund private sales below a minimum price.
  • Serial number or VIN. Ask the seller early for it (VIN is the vehicle identification number) so checks can start straight away.

How do balloons, GST and tax work on equipment loans?

A balloon (also called a residual) is a lump sum you leave to pay at the end of the loan, which lowers the repayments in the meantime.

  • Balloon size. For newer trucks, 20–40% is often available, lender-set and indicative.
  • Balloon risk. The machine may be worth less than the balloon when the loan ends.
  • GST and depreciation. These depend on the loan structure and your business. Speak to your accountant before choosing between a chattel mortgage, lease or hire purchase. We arrange the loan; we do not give tax advice.

Example structure — illustrative

Tipper truck and excavator, Toowoomba QLD

Situation: A civil contractor adding a 12-tonne excavator and a second tipper truck to the business, bought from a dealer and a private seller.

Constraint: The tipper was nine years old, and the contractor had two existing equipment loans with different lenders.

Structure: One lender funded both items under a chattel mortgage. The excavator had a 20% balloon over five years; the used tipper had no balloon and a four-year term. Loan from a lender that isn't a bank, secured by the machines only.

What lenders ask for

  • Make, model, year and serial number or VIN of each machine
  • A supplier invoice or sale contract, or a quote from the dealer
  • For used machines: hours or kilometres and, for private sales, a PPSR search
  • ABN, business activity statements for the last 12 months, and identification for each director
  • Details of existing equipment loans, if any, with the current balance and repayment
  • A business bank statement history, usually three to six months

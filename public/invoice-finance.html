What is invoice finance?

Invoice finance is a facility where a funder advances you cash against invoices you've issued to customers but not yet been paid for. You repay when the customer pays. Debtor finance is the same thing under another name.

You'll see both terms in Australia. It's a form of working capital finance, aimed at the gap created by payment terms. The basic steps are:

  1. You invoice your customer on payment terms, for example 30 or 60 days.
  2. The funder advances an agreed percentage of the invoice value. That percentage is the advance rate.
  3. Your customer pays the invoice on or after the due date.
  4. The funder releases the rest of the invoice value, less its charges.

For the other kinds of finance we arrange, see our page on business loans.

What is the difference between factoring and invoice discounting?

With factoring, the funder collects payment from your customers and often runs the collections for you. With invoice discounting, you keep collecting from your customers and the funder stays in the background.

Who collects from customersThe funderYou
Do customers knowUsually yes, the facility is disclosedUsually no, the facility is confidential
Who runs the sales ledgerThe funder often doesYou do
Often suitsBusinesses that want help with collectionsBusinesses with their own credit control in place

In Australia the labels blur, and a funder may use one name for either product. Ask each funder how its facility works instead of relying on the name.

How can an invoice finance facility be set up?

Three choices shape a facility: disclosed or confidential, whole ledger or selective, and recourse or non-recourse. Each trades control, cost and risk.

  • Disclosed or confidential. In a disclosed facility your customers are told and pay the funder. In a confidential one they keep paying as usual and aren't told.
  • Whole ledger or selective. A whole-ledger facility finances all invoices to approved customers. A selective facility lets you choose which customers or invoices to fund, down to a single invoice. Selective gives control but can cost more per invoice.
  • Recourse or non-recourse. With recourse, you carry the loss if a customer doesn't pay. With non-recourse, the funder takes some of that risk, usually for approved customers and set reasons, and it can cost more.

Funders set their own terms for each of these, so the same label can mean different things from one funder to the next.

How is an invoice finance facility sized?

A funder lends a percentage of your approved unpaid invoices, not a percentage of your sales. A rough guide is annual turnover times payment days divided by 365, then the advance rate applied.

The example below assumes customers pay on 45-day terms and the advance rate is 80%. The formula is turnover × 45 ÷ 365 × 80%.

$30mAbout $3.7mAbout $3.0m

Take the $10m row. $10,000,000 × 45 ÷ 365 is about $1,232,877 of invoices outstanding. At 80%, that's about $986,000, shown as $990,000. The $5m row sits just under the $500k we usually start from, and is shown for scale.

A real limit also depends on who your customers are, how concentrated your sales are among a few of them, how old the invoices get, and how many are disputed. Funders leave out invoices that don't meet their rules.

Which businesses does invoice finance suit?

It suits businesses that invoice other businesses on payment terms and have to pay their own costs before they get paid.

  • Labour hire. Wages go out weekly while clients pay later.
  • Transport. Fuel, drivers and tolls are paid before the freight invoice is.
  • Wholesale. Stock is paid for up front and sold on terms.
  • Manufacturing. Materials and labour are paid for before the buyer pays.

It tends to fit less well where customers pay at the point of sale, because there are no invoices to borrow against. A transport operator often pairs invoice finance for running costs with truck finance for the vehicles.

What does invoice finance cost?

There are usually two types of charge: a service or administration fee, and a discount or interest charge on the money you draw. Funders set their own, so ask for every charge in writing.

  • Service or administration fee. Pays for running the facility and the ledger. It can be based on turnover or on the invoices financed.
  • Discount or interest charge. Charged on the funds advanced, for the time they're outstanding.
  • Other charges. These can include set-up, review or audit fees, and fees for extras such as credit insurance or collections.

Compare facilities on the total cost across a typical month, not on one charge alone.

What happens if my customer does not pay?

It depends on whether the facility is recourse or non-recourse. With recourse, you repay the funder or swap the invoice. With non-recourse, the funder carries some of the loss, but usually only for approved customers and set reasons.

Either way, funders watch for slow payers. They may stop advancing against overdue or disputed invoices, and may cap how much they lend against any one customer.

Read how the contract defines non-payment, and what happens after a dispute. Tell the funder early if a customer is running late.

Will my customers know I use invoice finance?

Only if the facility is disclosed. In a confidential facility, customers keep paying as they do now and aren't told.

In a disclosed facility the funder usually notifies your customers, and they pay into the funder's account. Handled politely, that's an administrative change. The risk is collections calls that sound like debt chasing, so ask how the funder contacts customers.

If customer relationships are sensitive for your business, say so up front. It can decide which type of facility suits you.

How does invoice finance sit alongside a term loan or asset finance?

Invoice finance funds the invoices, while other facilities fund other things. In a larger deal the facilities have to fit together, because several lenders may want a claim over the same assets.

  • Different jobs. Invoice finance covers working capital, a term loan covers a purchase or a refinance, and equipment finance covers vehicles and machines.
  • Overlapping security. An invoice funder takes the unpaid invoices. A term lender may want a general security agreement over all business assets. The two have to agree who ranks first.
  • Existing debt. An overdraft secured over your debtors may need to be paid out or released before invoice finance starts. See business loan refinancing and business debt consolidation.

A chattel mortgage sits on the vehicle itself, so it rarely clashes with a claim over invoices. For wider context on business lending, see business lending in the September quarter 2026.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between factoring and invoice discounting?

With factoring, the funder collects from your customers. With invoice discounting, you keep collecting and customers usually aren't told. In Australia the labels blur, so ask each funder how its facility works rather than relying on the name.

What does invoice finance cost?

Usually two types of charge: a service or administration fee, and a discount or interest charge on the funds you draw. Other fees can apply, such as set-up or review fees. Get every charge in writing before you sign.

Can I finance a single invoice?

Yes, some funders offer selective or single invoice finance, where you choose which invoices to fund. It gives you control but can cost more per invoice than a whole-ledger facility, and funders set their own minimums.

Is invoice finance recourse or non-recourse?

Both exist. With recourse you carry the loss if a customer doesn't pay. With non-recourse the funder takes some of that risk, usually for approved customers and set reasons, and it can cost more.

What happens if my customer does not pay?

With recourse, you repay the funder or replace the invoice. With non-recourse, the funder may carry the loss where the contract covers that kind of non-payment. Either way, funders can stop advancing against overdue or disputed invoices.

Will my customers know I use invoice finance?

Only if the facility is disclosed. In a confidential facility customers keep paying as they do now. In a disclosed one they are told to pay the funder.

Is invoice finance the same as debtor finance?

Yes. Both terms describe borrowing against unpaid customer invoices. The product on offer matters more than the name, so check whether it is factoring or discounting, and whole ledger or selective.

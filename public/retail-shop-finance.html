How do lenders view buying a retail shop?

With more caution than an industrial unit, because a shop is only as safe as its rent.

  • Lower LVR. Lenders usually cap the LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value) at around 55–65% for retail. That range is lender-set and indicative, and it moves with the details.
  • Foot traffic. Online shopping has made some lenders wary of retail that depends on passing foot traffic. They are far more comfortable with daily-need shops, such as a pharmacy, a café or a hairdresser.
  • Valuation matters more. The lender-ordered property valuation (a value check the lender commissions) tests the rent against nearby shops. A valuer may mark the shop down if the rent looks too high.
  • Wider picture. This sits within wider commercial property finance.

Does it matter if the shop is in a strip, standalone or a shopping centre?

Yes, the format changes both the loan amount and the number of lenders who will look at it.

  • Strip shops. A shop on a busy main street is the easiest to fund. One in a quiet side street, or at the weak end of a strip with several empty shops, is not.
  • Standalone shops. Judged mostly on the single tenant. A national pharmacy or a supermarket franchise supports a good loan. A tenant with no track record may not.
  • Shopping centre lots. Usually a strata lot (a unit within a building owned in shares). Lenders look at the centre's tenant mix, the building's repair fund and whether the centre owner controls the trading rules. Some decline centre lots altogether.
  • Flat above. Lenders treat this differently again. See our page on shop-top and mixed-use property finance.

What if the shop is empty or the lease is about to end?

An empty shop can still be funded, but expect a lower loan and a lender that wants to see your plan.

  • Lease length. Lenders look at WALE (weighted average lease expiry — the average time left on the leases). A shop with 6 years left on its lease looks very different to one with 8 months left.
  • Common traps. A lease close to the end, a rent-free period that hides weak rent, and a tenant that is a company linked to the seller.
  • Outgoings. Check who pays them (council rates, water and building insurance). A lease where the tenant pays them leaves more income for the lender to count.
  • Trading it yourself. If you plan to trade from the shop, the lender will assess whether your business can cover the repayments.

Can I buy the shop through my super fund, or buy the business as well?

Often yes to both, with conditions.

  • Super fund purchase. A self-managed super fund (SMSF) can buy a shop that your business then leases from the fund at a market rent.
  • Strict rules. See SMSF commercial property loans first.
  • Buying the business too. If you are also buying the trading business inside the shop, the purchase is assessed in two parts: the property and the business. See business acquisition finance for how the second part works.

What lenders ask for

  • The contract of sale and the current lease, including rent reviews, outgoings and any rent-free period
  • Tenant details: who they are, how long they have traded, and recent rent payment history
  • Your last two years of business and personal tax returns, and your statement of assets and debts
  • For strata or centre lots: the levy notice, the repair fund statement and the by-laws
  • The lender-ordered valuation, which we organise once you choose a lender

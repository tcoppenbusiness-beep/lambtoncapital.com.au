Why do lenders like warehouses and industrial units?

Because they have steady demand from tenants and buyers, and they tend to hold value.

  • Most lender choice. Industrial is the commercial property type with the widest lender interest, so you often have the most choice of lenders and the best chance of a higher LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value).
  • Typical borrowing. With a mainstream lender, an owner-occupier can often borrow up to 65–75% of the valuation. Those figures are lender-set and indicative, and they fall for older or unusual buildings.
  • Buying your current unit. If you are buying the unit your business already rents, the lender can see your trading history from that site, and moving costs and downtime are off the table.

What makes a warehouse worth more, or less, to a lender?

Use, access and the physical building: the lender-ordered property valuation (done by a valuer the lender picks, paid for by you) checks four things.

  • Zoning and permitted use. The property must be zoned for what you do there, and your use needs council approval if it is unusual, such as food production or vehicle repair.
  • Clearance height. Modern racking and forklifts need 8–10 metres, and an old shed with a 4-metre roof is worth less to most tenants.
  • Hardstand and access. A concrete yard and room for a B-double (a long semi-trailer combination) to turn matter for logistics buyers.
  • Power and loading docks. Three-phase power and dock-height doors add value, while a single roller door does not.

What about contamination and older industrial sites?

Expect extra checks on sites with a history of fuel, chemicals, metal work or dry cleaning.

  • Environmental report. Lenders may ask for one (a specialist's check for soil or groundwater contamination) before they approve.
  • If contamination is found. Some lenders decline outright, others lower the LVR, and a few will lend if there is a clean-up plan.
  • Order it early. It can take weeks, and a negative result can affect your contract.
  • Extending the building. To extend or add a shed after buying, a construction loan can sit alongside the purchase loan. The lender assesses the finished property value, not just today's.

Example structure — illustrative

Industrial unit, Wacol QLD

Situation: A freight and storage business buying the 1,100 m² unit it had rented for six years, to stop rent increases and secure space for a second racking line.

Constraint: The unit was built in 1998 with a 6-metre clearance and an older fuel-storage history on the neighbouring lot, which triggered an environmental check.

Structure: Loan from a major bank, secured by the unit. A clean environmental report allowed the lender to keep the LVR at 70%. Forklifts were funded separately with a chattel mortgage (a loan secured by the equipment itself).

What lenders ask for

  • The contract of sale, title search, and zoning certificate showing your use is permitted
  • Your business's last two years of financials, plus a trading history from the current site if you rent it now
  • A current lease and rent history if you are buying the unit you lease
  • An environmental or contamination report for older industrial sites or those near fuel or chemical uses
  • Building details: age, clearance height, hardstand, and any council approvals for extensions
  • Proof of deposit and the lender's own property valuation

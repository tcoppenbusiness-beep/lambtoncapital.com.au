What is a business overdraft?

A business overdraft is a credit limit attached to your everyday business account. When your balance runs out, you can keep paying from the account up to the limit. You repay by paying money back in.

Interest is usually charged on the amount you've drawn, not on the whole limit. It's meant for short swings in cash, such as paying wages a week before a customer pays you.

The lender reviews the limit from time to time and may change it. Ask whether it can be called in on demand. See our page on business loans for the other kinds of business finance we arrange.

What is a business line of credit?

A business line of credit is a separate limit you can draw from, repay and draw from again. It usually sits apart from your everyday account, and you move money across when you need it.

It often has a set term with a review or expiry date. Interest is charged on the drawn balance, and some lenders add a fee on the whole limit. It can suit larger amounts, planned peaks and needs that keep coming back.

Ask whether the limit stays the same or steps down over time. For how both facilities fit with loans and invoice funding, see working capital finance.

What is the difference between an overdraft and a line of credit?

An overdraft sits on your everyday account, while a line of credit is a separate facility. Both are drawn, repaid and drawn again, and interest is charged on what you draw. The differences are in the terms.

Is a business overdraft secured or unsecured?

Both exist. Smaller overdrafts are sometimes unsecured, but at $500k and above lenders often ask for security.

  • Secured. Backed by property, or by a general security agreement, which is the lender's claim over the business's assets if the loan isn't repaid.
  • Unsecured. No specific asset is pledged. These tend to be smaller and cost more, and directors may still be asked for personal guarantees.

A personal guarantee puts your own assets behind the business debt. Speak to your solicitor before you sign one.

How do lenders set the limit?

Lenders set it from what the business earns and collects, then check what backs it. There's no single formula, and each lender sets its own limits.

  • Cash flow. Whether trading cash can cover interest, shown in financial statements and bank statements.
  • How the account runs. Whether it swings up and down, or sits near the limit all year.
  • Debtors and stock. What customers owe you and what you hold.
  • Security. What backs the facility, its value, and any other lender with a claim over the same assets.
  • Existing debt and tax. Other facilities, repayment history, and any tax debt.

What if my overdraft is always fully drawn?

An overdraft that never comes back below its limit is often called hard core debt. It means the business is using the facility as long-term funding, not for short swings in cash.

That causes two problems. You have no spare room when cash gets tight, and a lender reviewing the limit may read it as a sign the business needs permanent funding.

Moving part of it to a term loan can make sense when:

  • The balance has stayed close to the limit for months.
  • The business can carry fixed repayments over a set period.
  • You want the overdraft free again for day-to-day use.

A term loan swaps flexibility for set repayments, and it can bring new fees and security. See business loan refinancing for replacing existing facilities, and business debt consolidation if several debts are involved.

What is an unapproved or excess overdraft, and why does it matter?

An unapproved overdraft is when your account goes below zero with no agreed limit, or past the limit you have. It's also called an excess overdraft. It matters because it can cost more and it shows on your statements.

  • Cost. The lender can charge fees or a higher interest charge on the excess.
  • Payments. A payment can be declined if there's no room left.
  • Assessment. A new lender reading your bank statements will see it, and may take it as a sign of stretched cash flow.

If it keeps happening, ask for a limit that fits before you go over, not after.

How do I increase my limit?

Ask the lender that holds the facility first, with up-to-date numbers. If it says no, or the limit still won't be enough, a different lender may look at it differently.

  1. Work out how much you need, what for, and for how long.
  2. Gather current financial statements and recent bank statements.
  3. Say what extra security you can offer, if any.
  4. Ask in writing, and ask what the lender will need to decide.

A higher limit usually means a fresh assessment, and it can mean a new valuation and new fees. Lenders decide each time, and we can't promise an outcome.

What will a lender ask for?

Lenders want proof the business can carry the facility and proof of what backs it. Have these ready.

  • ID for every director and guarantor
  • Financial statements and tax returns, most recent two years
  • Current-year management accounts
  • Recent BAS (business activity statements, your quarterly goods and services tax returns) and business bank statements
  • An Australian Taxation Office (ATO) portal statement
  • Aged debtor and creditor listings (what customers owe you and what you owe suppliers), where relevant
  • Details of any assets offered as security, including existing loans

Missing a few items? Send what you have and we'll tell you what's still needed.

What can I do when a bank says no?

A no from one lender isn't the end. Each lender has its own policy, so the reason matters more than the answer.

  • Ask why. Tax debt, weak records and thin security are fixable. Some reasons aren't.
  • Try another type of lender. A different bank, or a lender that isn't a bank, may assess the same numbers differently.
  • Change the security. Offering property or business assets can change the answer.
  • Change the facility. Borrowing against unpaid customer invoices is invoice finance, and a vehicle or machine is asset finance.
  • Ask for less. A smaller limit may be easier to approve than the one you asked for.

We can't promise an outcome with any of these.

Frequently asked questions

Overdraft or line of credit, which is better?

Neither is better for everyone. An overdraft suits day-to-day swings in cash on your main account. A line of credit suits larger or planned needs and keeps the borrowing separate. Which one you're offered depends on the lender and your numbers.

Can I get an unsecured business overdraft?

Sometimes, mostly at smaller limits. At $500k and above, lenders often ask for security, such as property or a general security agreement over business assets. Directors may be asked for personal guarantees too.

How is interest charged on a business overdraft?

Interest is charged on the amount you've drawn, not on the whole limit. Some lenders also charge a fee on the limit itself, so ask for every charge in writing.

Is an overdraft repayable on demand?

Some are. The terms say whether the lender can call the facility in at any time or only under set conditions. Read that clause before you sign, and ask about it if it isn't clear.

How do I increase my overdraft limit?

Ask your current lender, with up-to-date financials and bank statements, and say how much you need and why. Offering more security can help. Lenders decide each time, and we can't promise an outcome.

What is the difference between an overdraft and a business loan?

An overdraft is a limit you draw on and repay as cash moves through the account. A business loan is a lump sum repaid over a set period with regular repayments. A loan suits a one-off purpose, and an overdraft suits timing gaps.

What is an unapproved overdraft?

It's when your account goes below zero with no agreed limit, or past the limit you have. It can attract fees or a higher charge, payments can be declined, and a new lender will see it on your bank statements.

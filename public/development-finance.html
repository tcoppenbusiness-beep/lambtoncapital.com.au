What does development finance cover?

It covers land bank facilities and construction facilities (a facility is the loan or credit line itself), funding a project from site purchase to completed stock.

  • Land bank facility. Holds a site while you wait for approvals. Usually a lower LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value) than a standard purchase, because the site has no income and no finished value yet.
  • Construction facility. Funds the build itself, paid out in stages as work is certified.
  • Project types. Residential, commercial or mixed-use — a small townhouse project, a retail and office building, or a shed with an attached showroom.
  • Not other loans. Separate from buying a finished commercial property. Building or extending premises you'll occupy? See our construction loan on your own premises page.

How much can a project borrow?

Lenders cap borrowing at a set percentage of gross realisation value (GRV) — what the finished project is expected to sell for — or of total development cost (TDC).

  • What TDC includes. Land, construction, professional fees and contingency added together.
  • Presales or pre-leases. Most lenders want minimum presales (units sold off the plan before construction) or pre-leases (tenants signed before the build is finished) in place before the first payment from the loan.
  • A guide only. Caps, the GRV and TDC mix and the presale minimum vary with lender policy, project type, location and the developer's experience.

What are capitalised interest, QS reports and drawdowns?

Capitalised interest means interest during construction is added to the loan instead of paid monthly, so you aren't making repayments before the project earns income.

  • QS report. A quantity surveyor (QS — a cost specialist who checks the build budget and progress), not part of the project, reports on the cost to build.
  • Drawdowns. The QS certifies each stage of work before the lender releases the next drawdown (a staged payment from the loan), so the lender doesn't pay for work not done.
  • Mezzanine finance. Bigger or riskier projects sometimes add mezzanine finance (a second, higher-cost loan that sits behind the main loan). It cuts the equity — the developer's own money — needed up front, but costs more and carries more risk.

Two worked examples

Example structure — illustrative

Townhouse development, Maitland NSW

Situation: A small builder-developer constructing eight townhouses on a site already owned outright.

Constraint: The lender's policy required a minimum level of presales before the first construction payment from the loan. The builder also needed the land bank period (holding the site before building) funded first.

Structure: A construction loan from a non-bank lender (one that isn't a bank), sized against TDC. It was paid out in stages as the QS certified progress, with interest added to the loan through the build.

Loan $3.6m · LVR 65% of TDC · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 42

Example structure — illustrative

Mixed-use building, Toowoomba QLD

Situation: A developer building ground-floor retail with offices above, with one tenant pre-committed.

Constraint: Only some tenants were signed before the build, which limited the lenders willing to lend against GRV.

Structure: A specialist development lender, sized against GRV. A mezzanine loan (a second, higher-cost loan) covered part of the equity gap, and interest was added to the loan.

Loan $6.2m · LVR 60% of GRV · Lender tier Specialist · Days to settle 51

What does business finance cover?

Business finance funds running or growing a trading business, separate from a property purchase.

  • What it spans. Short-term cash flow support, buying another business, restructuring existing debt, and longer-term equipment or growth capital.
  • How it's secured. Most facilities (the loans or credit lines themselves) are secured by property, other assets, a general security agreement (the lender's claim over the business's assets if the loan isn't repaid), or a mix.
  • Right structure. It depends on what the money is for and how the business is trading.
  • Facility expiring? If that, rather than new funding, is the driver, see commercial loan refinance.

Working capital

Short-term funding for stock, wages or a seasonal cash gap, usually a limit you can draw on, repay and draw again.

Business acquisition

Funding to buy an existing business, a competitor, or a franchise.

Debt restructure

Combining several loans into one, on better terms or with longer to repay.

Term loans

A loan for a fixed amount, repaid over a set period, for equipment, fit-out or a defined growth project.

Overdrafts

A credit limit backed by the business or a property, which you draw on as trading needs it.

Trade & debtor finance

Borrowing against unpaid customer invoices or imported stock, so cash flow doesn't wait on payment terms.

How do lenders decide between an overdraft, a term loan and trade finance?

The facility is tailored to match what is actually being funded. An overdraft is suitable for a cash flow gap that occurs intermittently; a term loan is appropriate for a one-off purchase which has a clear repayment date; trade or debtor finance is suitable for a business that is waiting for invoices or has import stock rather than one that is generally short of cash. Lenders also take into account a company's trading history, the amount of debt it already has, and the type of security being offered — a facility secured by property or one that has a general security agreement (a claim over the business's assets) over the business is usually priced more favourably than an unsecured one.

Example structure — illustrative

Working capital facility, Wollongong NSW

Situation: A wholesaler needing stock funding ahead of a seasonal peak, trading for two years under the current business structure.

Constraint: Cash flow was seasonal, and a standard assessment for an overdraft undervalued it.

Structure: Credit line you can draw, repay and draw again, from a lender that isn't a bank, secured by a general security agreement, 12-month review.

Loan $650k · LVR n/a · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 18

Example structure — illustrative

Business acquisition, Bendigo VIC

Situation: A trades operator buying out a competitor's client book and equipment on retirement.

Constraint: Goodwill (the value of the business beyond its physical assets) made up most of the purchase price, which limited how many banks would lend.

Structure: Loan from a mid-tier bank (smaller than the big four), repaid over 10 years, secured by both the business and a property.

Loan $1.2m · LVR 55% · Lender tier Mid-tier bank · Days to settle 42

Example structure — illustrative

Debt restructure, Toowoomba QLD

Situation: A manufacturer carrying three separate facilities across two lenders after a period of rapid growth.

Constraint: Several loans were backed by overlapping assets and their terms didn't line up, adding cost and complexity.

Structure: One loan from a lender that isn't a bank, replacing the existing debt, 7-year term, one set of assets as security.

Loan $2.4m · LVR 60% · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 35

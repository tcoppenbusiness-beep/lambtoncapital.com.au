Construction finance

Who is construction finance for?

It's for an owner-occupier or investor building a single commercial premises, or extending one they already have.

  • Examples. A business building its own warehouse, or an investor adding a second tenancy to an existing site.
  • Not development finance. Development finance funds a multi-unit or multi-lot project built to sell or lease to third parties.
  • Paid in stages. The lender pays in progress payments (the loan paid out in stages) against a fixed-price contract, not the full loan at settlement (when the loan funds are paid and the deal completes).
  • Why stages. Until the build is done, there is no finished building for the lender to claim if the loan isn't repaid.

How are progress payments released?

Each payment is released against a claim from the builder, matched to a stage in the fixed-price contract — slab, frame, lock-up, fit-out and so on.

  • Inspections. Most lenders send an outside inspector to confirm each stage is complete before releasing funds, so no one pays ahead of the work.
  • Interest during the build. Usually charged only on the money paid out so far.
  • Capitalising interest. Some lenders let you capitalise it (add it to the loan instead of paying monthly), so it doesn't come out of your pocket during construction.

What do lenders check on the builder and the contract?

They check the builder's licence, insurances and track record, and that the contract is fixed-price rather than cost-plus.

  • Why fixed-price. An open-ended contract makes the total cost harder to assess.
  • "As if complete" valuation. The lender-ordered property valuation shows what the property will be worth once built, not what the site is worth today.
  • Loan limit. That valuation, alongside the contract price, sets the most the lender will fund.

Two worked examples

Example structure — illustrative

Owner-occupier warehouse build, Bathurst NSW

Situation: A logistics business building a new 1,200 m² warehouse on land it already owned, to move out of a leased site.

Constraint: The business needed interest added to the loan through the build (capitalised), so cash flow wasn't strained before the move.

Structure: A construction loan from a mid-tier bank (a smaller bank, such as a regional bank), paid in stages against a fixed-price contract. Valued as if complete, with interest added to the loan.

Loan $2.3m · LVR 65% of as-if-complete value · Lender tier Mid-tier bank · Days to settle 34

Example structure — illustrative

Investor extension, Bunbury WA

Situation: An investor adding a second tenancy to an existing industrial site to lease separately.

Constraint: The extension had to be valued and funded on its finished, tenanted state, not the current partly-used site.

Structure: A non-bank lender (one that isn't a bank), paying in stages against builder claims, with outside progress inspections.

Loan $980k · LVR 60% of as-if-complete value · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 29

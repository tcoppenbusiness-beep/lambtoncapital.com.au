Why is childcare harder to finance than other property?

Because the building has one use and the income depends on a licence.

  • Few buyers. A purpose-built centre has few other buyers, so lenders value it cautiously.
  • Lower LVR. Indicative LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value) is often 50–65%. It is lender-set and varies by centre.
  • Few lenders. Only a small group of lenders are active in childcare. Several are not banks (funded privately or by investors).
  • Experience. The ones that do lend often want the buyer to have sector experience, or an operator already in place.

Is buying the building different from buying the centre?

Yes. They are two separate deals, and lenders assess them on different things.

  • Buying the building. A freehold purchase (you own the land and building) is judged like an investment property with one tenant, the operator. The lender looks at the lease, the rent per licensed place and who the operator is.
  • Buying the centre. This means buying a going business, so the lender tests the centre's trading profit and whether you can run it. That is closer to business acquisition finance.
  • Doing both. Some buyers own the building and operate the centre. That needs the lender to be comfortable with the business and the property together.

What do lenders check in a childcare centre?

They check the licence, the number of children and the quality rating. Details differ by state.

  • Licensed places. The approved number of children the centre may care for at one time.
  • Occupancy. How many of those places are filled, and for how many days.
  • Provider and rating. The approved provider (the company or person authorised to run the service) and the centre's rating under the National Quality Standard (NQS). A centre rated as not meeting the standard, or under a compliance action, is much harder to fund.
  • Freehold leases. A long lease to a large national operator is a strong point. A short lease to a first-time operator is not.

Can I fund a new centre from scratch?

Yes, but it is a project loan, not a purchase loan.

  • Land and approvals first. Buying the land and gaining council approval comes first. Development finance covers this stage.
  • Then the build. Once building starts, construction finance pays the builder in stages as the work is completed.
  • Operator signed up. Lenders will usually want one before they agree to lend.

Childcare centre, Toowoomba QLD

Situation: An investor buying the freehold of a 90-place centre, leased to a national operator on a 12-year lease.

Constraint: Few lenders were active in regional childcare, and the lender wanted proof the lease rent was in line with local rents.

Structure: Loan from a lender that is not a bank, interest-only for 3 years then repaid over 22 years, secured by the centre and a smaller property owned by the buyer.

What lenders ask for

  • The licence and approved provider details, plus the latest quality rating and any compliance notices
  • Occupancy figures by month for the last two years, and the number of licensed places
  • The lease to the operator, or if you are buying the business, the contract of sale and last three years of accounts
  • Council approvals and plans, including parking and outdoor play space
  • Your experience in childcare, or a plan to appoint an experienced operator

What is working capital finance and when does a business use it?

Working capital finance is borrowing to cover the gap between money going out and money coming in. A business uses it to pay suppliers, wages and rent while it waits for customers to pay.

It's different from a loan for a building or a machine. You aren't buying something that lasts. You're funding the timing of your cash. Working capital loans and lines of credit are the common forms.

A business tends to look for it when:

  • It buys stock ahead of a busy season and gets paid after the season ends.
  • Customers pay on 30, 60 or 90 day terms, but wages and suppliers are due sooner.
  • A large contract needs materials or labour paid for up front.
  • An importer pays an overseas supplier before the goods arrive and sell. See trade finance.
  • Growth uses cash before it earns any.

We usually work with facilities from $500k to $5m. See our page on business loans for the other kinds of finance we arrange, including acquisitions and restructures.

What are the main types of working capital finance?

There are four main types: a business overdraft, a line of credit, a short-term or term working capital loan, and invoice finance. They differ in how you draw the money, how you repay it and what backs it.

TypeHow it worksSuitsOften backed by
Business overdraftA limit on your business account. You draw and repay as money moves through the account.Day-to-day swings in cashProperty or a general security agreement over business assets
Line of creditA set limit you can draw, repay and draw again, usually separate from your everyday account.Needs that keep coming back, or planned peaksProperty, business assets or both
Short-term or term working capital loanA lump sum repaid over a set period.A single need with a clear end date, such as a seasonal stock buildVaries by lender and size
Invoice financeBorrowing against unpaid customer invoices. See our invoice finance page.Businesses that invoice other businesses on termsThe unpaid invoices, sometimes with other assets

Many businesses use more than one. A line of credit for the peak season and a term loan for a one-off purchase is one pairing. If the need is a vehicle or machine, that's asset finance, not working capital.

What is the difference between an overdraft and a line of credit?

A business overdraft is a limit attached to your everyday account. A line of credit is a separate limit with its own terms. Both let you borrow, repay and borrow again, up to the limit.

  • Overdraft. The limit sits on your transaction account, so money in and out moves the balance. It's simple to use. Check whether the lender can call it in on demand.
  • Line of credit. A separate facility you draw from by transfer. It often has set terms and a review date, and it can suit larger amounts.
  • Cost. Interest is usually charged on what you draw. Some lenders also charge a fee on the whole limit, so ask.

For a closer look at both facilities, see our guide to the business overdraft and line of credit.

Loan or line of credit? Choose a loan for a single amount with a clear end date, such as one seasonal stock build. Choose a line of credit when the need keeps recurring. Lenders decide which they'll offer, based on how your cash moves.

Is working capital finance secured or unsecured?

Both exist. At $500k and above, lenders often ask for security. Unsecured facilities tend to be smaller and cost more.

Security can be one or more of these:

  • Property, owned by the business or by a director.
  • A general security agreement, which is the lender's claim over the business's assets if the loan isn't repaid.
  • Unpaid customer invoices, in the case of invoice finance.
  • Personal guarantees from the directors.

A personal guarantee puts your own assets behind the business debt. Speak to your solicitor before you sign one. If your existing debt is already secured on your home, see business loan refinancing for how security can change when you move.

How do lenders size a working capital facility of $500k and above?

Lenders size it from what the business earns and collects, then check what backs it. There's no single formula, and each lender sets its own limits.

FactorWhat the lender looks at
Cash flowWhether trading cash can cover interest and repayments, shown in financial statements and bank statements.
DebtorsHow much customers owe you, how old those invoices are, and whether a few customers make up most of it.
SecurityWhat backs the facility, its value, and any existing lender with a claim over the same assets.
Trading historyHow long the business has traded under its current structure, how steady sales are, and how seasonal.
Existing debt and taxOther facilities, repayment history, and any tax debt or payment arrangements.

A seasonal business can look weaker than it is if a lender only sees its slowest quarter. We'd show the full year and explain the pattern up front.

What documents will a lender ask for?

Lenders want proof the business can carry the facility and proof of what backs it. Have these ready.

  • ID for every director and guarantor
  • Financial statements and tax returns, most recent two years
  • Current-year management accounts
  • Recent BAS (business activity statements, your quarterly goods and services tax returns) and business bank statements
  • An Australian Taxation Office (ATO) portal statement
  • Aged debtor and creditor listings (what customers owe you and what you owe suppliers), where relevant
  • Details of any assets offered as security, including existing loans

Missing a few items? Send what you have and we'll tell you what's still needed.

Can I get working capital finance with an Australian Taxation Office (ATO) payment plan?

Sometimes, but it makes the assessment harder. Lenders treat tax debt and payment arrangements as part of the overall assessment, and policies differ.

Some lenders check that you're keeping up with the plan. Others won't lend while a tax debt is overdue. We can't promise an outcome.

If tax debt is one of several debts, business debt consolidation explains how lenders look at the whole picture. If the business can't service its debts, more borrowing only moves the problem. The Small Business Debt Helpline, run by Financial Counselling Australia, is a free place to start.

How quickly can working capital finance be arranged?

It depends on the lender and the security. Business finance through us typically settles in 2 to 8 weeks. That's an indicative range, not a promise.

These are the steps that take the time:

  • Gather documents. This is the part you control, so start here.
  • Send us the deal. Five short questions, with no login.
  • Assessment and shortlist. We reply in writing within two business days.
  • Lender assessment. The lender reviews your numbers and security, and may order a valuation if property is offered.
  • Documents and settlement. You sign, security is registered, and the facility becomes available.

Starting early helps. A valuation or a document request can add time, and each lender sets its own pace.

It depends on the lender, but we usually work with facilities from $500k to $5m. The limit comes from your cash flow, debtors, security and trading history, and each lender sets its own rules.

Is working capital finance secured or unsecured?

Both exist, but at $500k and above lenders often ask for security. That can be property, a general security agreement over business assets, unpaid invoices, or a mix. Directors are often asked for personal guarantees too.

Should I get a working capital loan or a line of credit?

A loan suits a single amount with a clear end date. A line of credit suits a need that keeps coming back, because you can draw, repay and draw again. Which one you're offered depends on the lender.

What is the difference between an overdraft and a line of credit?

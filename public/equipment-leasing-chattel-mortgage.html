How does a chattel mortgage work?

A chattel mortgage is a loan to buy equipment or a vehicle, secured by that item, with you owning the asset from day one.

  • Ownership. You own the asset from settlement (when the loan funds are paid and the deal completes), so it counts as a business asset in your books.
  • The lender's claim. The lender takes a mortgage over it as security (a claim over the asset if the loan isn't repaid), registered on the Personal Property Securities Register (PPSR), a national register of debts secured against assets.
  • GST and depreciation. These follow from ownership. Depreciation is the asset's value written down over time for tax, and includes whether an instant asset write-off (an immediate tax deduction for eligible assets) applies.
  • Versus a lease or rental. There, the financier owns the asset and you pay to use it. See asset & equipment finance for how a chattel mortgage compares with leasing, rental and hire purchase more broadly.
  • Tax advice. GST and depreciation treatment is a question for your accountant, not something we advise on — this page describes how the finance is structured, not its tax effect.

What's a balloon payment and how does it change repayments?

A balloon is a lump sum left owing at the end of the term, and it lowers the regular repayment.

  • How it's sized. Against what the asset is expected to be worth at that point, so you're not paying off its full value over the term.
  • Worked example. On a $200,000 asset over a five-year chattel mortgage with a 30% balloon, roughly $140,000 is repaid over the term and around $60,000 falls due at the end.
  • Paying it out. You can pay it out, refinance it (replace it with a new loan), or cover it by trading the asset in.
  • Bigger or smaller. A larger balloon means lower repayments but more to manage at the end. A smaller one (or none) means higher repayments but nothing owing at the end.
  • Finding the balance. It depends on how long you'll keep the asset and what it's likely to be worth then. Figures are illustrative; actual terms are lender-set on the day.

Two worked examples

Example structure — illustrative

Concrete pump purchase, Ballarat VIC

Situation: A concreting business buying a new boom pump to bring a subcontracted task in-house.

Constraint: The business wanted to keep monthly repayments low in the first two years while a new contract ramped up.

Structure: Chattel mortgage, five-year term, 30% balloon, GST treatment referred to the client's accountant.

Medical imaging equipment, Adelaide SA

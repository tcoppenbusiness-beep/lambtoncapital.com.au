What is a commercial bridging loan?

A commercial bridging loan is a short-term loan, secured by commercial property, that covers the gap between paying for one thing and being repaid from another.

  • Short term. Terms typically run from a few months up to 24 months, and most are 12 months or less.
  • Buy before you sell. You settle on a new warehouse, shop or office while the old premises are still on the market.
  • Settle before the bank loan. You purchase now, then move to a longer-term loan once that approval is finished.
  • Clear a debt. It can pay a tax or creditor debt while a sale or refinance completes.
  • Secure a property at auction. Auction settlements are often 30 days or less, which is quicker than many standard loans can be arranged.

What is an exit strategy, and why does the lender focus on it?

Your exit is how the bridging loan gets repaid. It is the first thing a lender checks, because the loan has no long repayment history to rely on.

  • Sale of a property. The most common exit: the premises you are selling, or the one you are buying, is sold and the proceeds repay the loan.
  • Refinance to a bank. A longer-term loan (see commercial refinance) pays out the bridging loan once the property or business qualifies.
  • Business cash flow. Less common and harder to prove. The lender will want to see how the cash will arrive and when.
  • Evidence matters. A signed sale contract, an agent's appraisal or a lender's conditional approval makes the exit far more convincing than a plan on its own.

How is a bridging loan structured?

Most bridging loans are interest-only, secured by a mortgage over commercial property, with the interest added to the loan or paid up front rather than monthly.

  • Interest handling. Capitalised interest (added to the loan instead of paid monthly) or prepaid interest (taken from the loan at settlement) keeps cash free during the term.
  • Peak debt and end debt. Peak debt is the most you owe at any point. End debt is what remains after the exit. Lenders look at both, and capitalised interest pushes the end figure up.
  • First or second mortgage. A first mortgage ranks ahead of any other lender if the property is sold. A second mortgage sits behind an existing loan, so it is riskier for the lender and usually lends less.
  • Indicative LVR. LVR (loan-to-value ratio: the loan as a percentage of the property's value) is typically up to about 65 – 70% on a commercial first mortgage and lower for a second mortgage. Each lender sets its own limit.
  • Who lends. Mostly non-bank and private lenders (lenders that aren't banks, funded privately or by investors). Some banks offer bridging for buy-before-you-sell.

What does a bridging loan cost?

Bridging costs more than a standard bank loan, because the lender takes on a shorter timeframe, a tighter deadline and more risk.

  • Higher interest rate. Expect a rate above standard commercial lending. Rates are set by each lender and change with the market, so ask for current quotes.
  • Establishment fees. Lenders usually charge an upfront fee, plus legal and valuation costs paid by the borrower.
  • Why it can still make sense. You pay the higher cost for months, not years. If the alternative is losing a purchase or selling at a poor price, the cost can be worth it.
  • Add it up first. Compare the total cost of the loan with what the deal gains you, not just the rate.

What if the exit is delayed?

If the sale or refinance runs late, you can face default interest, extension fees and, in the worst case, a forced sale of the property.

  • Default interest. After the term ends, most lenders charge a higher rate until the loan is repaid.
  • Extension costs. A lender may agree to extend, usually for a fee and often at a higher rate.
  • Sale risk. A buyer can withdraw, or the property can sell for less than expected. Build in a buffer and set the sale price realistically.
  • Lender action. If the loan stays unpaid, the lender can sell the security (the property it holds a mortgage over) to recover what it is owed.
  • Plan B. A second exit, such as a lender's conditional approval to refinance, reduces this risk. We ask about it up front.

When is a bridging loan the wrong fit?

A bridging loan is the wrong tool when there is no clear exit, or when you need the money for years rather than months.

  • No clear exit. If repayment depends on hope rather than a sale, an approval or documented cash, most lenders will decline and the risk to you is high.
  • Long-term need. For a purchase you will hold, a standard loan is cheaper. See commercial property finance or commercial refinance.
  • Limited paperwork. If the issue is documents rather than timing, low-doc commercial loans (assessed on fewer financial documents) may fit better.
  • The timing gap is long. If the gap will stretch past 12 – 24 months, the interest cost adds up and a different structure is usually better.

Example structure — illustrative

Warehouse purchase, Beresfield NSW

Situation: A Hunter Valley trade supplier needed a larger warehouse in Beresfield and had to settle in three weeks, before its current Cardiff premises had sold.

Constraint: The business owned the Cardiff property outright, but a bank loan on the new warehouse would not be approved in time.

Structure: A 12-month first-mortgage bridging loan over the new warehouse and the Cardiff premises, interest capitalised. The exit is the Cardiff sale, already listed with an agent's appraisal. The rest of the price came from the business's cash.

Loan $2.2m · LVR 69% of the new warehouse (39% across both properties) · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 19

Example structure — illustrative

Industrial unit at auction, Moorabbin VIC

Situation: A building services business won an industrial unit at auction with a 30-day settlement.

Constraint: A longer-term bank loan was likely to take eight to ten weeks, longer than the contract allowed.

Structure: A 6-month first-mortgage bridging loan with interest prepaid. The exit is a refinance to a bank once the unit is in the business's name and the lender-ordered valuation is done.

Loan $1.2m · LVR 63% · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 14

What lenders ask for

  • A written exit plan, with evidence: a sale contract or agent's appraisal, or a lender's conditional approval
  • Details of the property you are buying and any property you are selling, including the contract of sale
  • Current loan statements for any existing mortgage over the security
  • Recent financial statements or BAS (Business Activity Statements, the quarterly GST return)
  • Proof of how the interest and fees will be covered, whether capitalised, prepaid or paid from cash

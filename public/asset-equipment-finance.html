What's the difference between a chattel mortgage, lease, rental and hire purchase?

The difference is who owns the asset: you do under a chattel mortgage or hire purchase (at the end), and the financier does under a lease or rental.

  • Chattel mortgage. A loan to buy the asset outright, secured by that asset (the lender can claim it if the loan isn't repaid). You own it from day one.
  • Finance lease. The lender buys and owns the asset while you pay to use it, with an option to buy or return it at the end.
  • Rental (operating lease). A use-and-return arrangement, often with maintenance included. Ownership never passes to you.
  • Hire purchase. You hire the asset on an agreed schedule and own it after the final payment, often a balloon (a lump sum left to pay at the end).
  • What most businesses choose. Most trades and transport businesses use a chattel mortgage or hire purchase for long-term equipment. See chattel mortgage in depth for a worked balloon example.

What's a balloon or residual, and does low-doc apply?

A balloon (on a chattel mortgage or hire purchase) or residual (on a lease) is a lump sum left owing at the end of the term.

  • Lower repayments. It lowers your regular repayment in exchange for that final payment.
  • How it's set. Usually against the asset's expected value at that point, so a shorter-lived asset carries a smaller balloon than one that holds its value well.
  • Low-doc option. Equipment finance is one of the easier low-doc categories (assessed on fewer financial documents). Many lenders accept an accountant's letter or recent bank statements instead of full financials, up to a limit that varies by lender and asset type.

Can I buy used equipment or from a private seller?

Yes, most lenders will fund used equipment and private sales, but their policy gets stricter as the asset gets older.

  • Tighter terms. Expect a shorter maximum term, a lower LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the asset's value) and a closer look at the asset's condition and remaining working life than on a new dealer purchase.
  • Private sales. These need extra checks, including a PPSR (Personal Property Securities Register) search, which confirms no other lender has a claim on the asset, before funds are released.
  • Where this applies. Yellow goods (earthmoving and construction machinery), trucks and trailers, medical equipment and IT assets.

Two worked examples

Example structure — illustrative

Excavator purchase, Rockhampton QLD

Situation: An earthmoving contractor buying a used 20-tonne excavator from a private seller to take on a new contract.

Constraint: The machine's age limited the term a mainstream lender would offer, and the private sale needed a clear PPSR check.

Structure: Chattel mortgage through an equipment-specialist lender, five-year term, 20% balloon.

Loan $210k · LVR n/a · Lender tier Equipment specialist · Days to settle 6

Example structure — illustrative

Fleet of delivery trucks, Dandenong VIC

Situation: A logistics business replacing four ageing delivery trucks with new vehicles from a dealer.

Constraint: The business wanted the deal assessed on recent bank statements rather than full financials, under the lender's low-doc limit.

Structure: Hire purchase across all four vehicles, seven-year term, with a different balloon for each truck based on its expected resale value.

Loan $480k · LVR n/a · Lender tier Bank · Days to settle 9

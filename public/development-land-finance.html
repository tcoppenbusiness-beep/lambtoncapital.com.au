How much can I borrow to buy development land?

Usually less than for a finished building: commonly about 50–65% of the land's value, and lower for raw land.

  • Lower than built property. Land earns no rent and has no building to fall back on, so lenders lend less against it. The figure is LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value).
  • Lender-set and indicative. Each lender sets its own limit. Land with zoning and a DA (development approval — council's permission to build your plan) in place sits at the top of the range. Raw land (englobo — a large, unsubdivided parcel) sits lower.
  • Banks and non-banks. Banks are often cautious about undeveloped land. Non-bank lenders (lenders that aren't banks) and private lenders do more of it, usually at a higher cost.
  • "As is" value. The lender lends against what the land is worth today, as it stands, not what the finished project could be worth. Your deposit covers the gap, plus stamp duty and legal costs.

Does it matter if the land has a DA or the right zoning?

Yes. Land that is already zoned for your plan, with a DA approved, is the easiest to fund.

  • Zoned and approved. The lender can see the plan is allowed and what it could be worth. That usually means more lender choice and a higher LVR.
  • A DA adds value, but not all of it counts. It can lift what the land is worth, yet lenders typically still lend on the current value and may want the valuer's view on it.
  • Zoned, no DA yet. Possible, but the lender is relying on you getting approval. Expect a lower LVR and more questions about your builder and your plans.
  • Rezoning plays. Buying land in the hope it will be rezoned is speculative. Most banks decline it, so it usually goes to non-bank or private lenders, with a low LVR and a short term.
  • Subject-to-DA contracts. A contract that depends on approval can reduce the amount you put at risk. Ask your solicitor how to word it.

How do I pay the loan while the land isn't earning?

By planning for holding costs (interest, rates and insurance) from day one, using one or more of the options below.

  • Interest-only. You pay only the interest each month and none of the loan itself, which keeps repayments lower while you wait for approval or finance to build.
  • Capitalised or prepaid interest. Some lenders add interest to the loan (capitalised interest) or set aside part of the loan to cover it in advance. That reduces cash out of your pocket but increases the debt, so the lender counts it in the LVR.
  • Servicing from other income. Many lenders want to see that your business or other income can cover the repayments. That is the lender's test of whether the repayments are affordable.
  • Rent from the site. An old house, a yard lease or farm income on the land helps. It shows some income and can soften the lender's view.
  • The other costs. Council rates, land tax, insurance, consultants' fees and DA costs all run while the land sits idle. Put them in a written budget; lenders ask for it.

What happens when I'm ready to build?

The land loan is usually refinanced into a construction or development loan that also pays for the build.

  • One new facility. The new lender pays out the land loan and then releases money in stages as the build reaches milestones. See development finance for multi-unit projects and construction finance for a single build.
  • Land equity counts. Lenders often treat the equity you hold in the land (its value less what you owe) as part of your contribution to the project cost. That can cut the cash you need to put in.
  • What they will check. Approved plans, a fixed-price building contract or a cost budget, and often a quantity surveyor's report (a cost specialist who checks the budget). Some lenders also ask for presales (units sold off the plan before building starts).
  • Plan the exit early. Tell your broker at the land stage if you will sell the site, build and hold, or build and sell. It decides which lender suits you at both steps and can save you refinancing costs.

When is a land loan not the right fit?

When your timing, the site or the plan doesn't match what land lenders want.

  • A very short settlement. If you need to settle in days, or you will sell or refinance within a few months, a commercial bridging loan may suit better.
  • Income-producing property. If the site already has tenants and you plan to keep it as it is, a standard commercial property loan looks at the rent and usually lends more.
  • No plan or no exit. Lenders don't like to fund land when the buyer cannot say what happens next. Land banking (holding land for future growth) is usually tough to fund with high LVRs.
  • A site you cannot hold. If the holding costs would strain your cash flow, tell us before you sign. It is easier to change the plan now than after exchange.

Situation: A small Hunter builder buying a 4,000 m² residential-zoned block with a DA approved for eight townhouses, to build and sell the dwellings.

Constraint: The builder wanted to keep cash for the build, and the bank they asked first would not lend on the land alone before construction started.

What lenders ask for

  • The contract of sale, or a signed offer, and the title search
  • The zoning certificate (in NSW, a section 10.7 planning certificate) showing what the council allows on the land
  • The DA approval and approved plans, or your concept plans and planning advice if there is no DA yet
  • The lender-ordered property valuation, on the land's current "as is" value
  • A holding-cost budget: interest, rates, land tax, insurance and consultants' fees until you build or sell
  • Your exit or next-stage plan: sell the site, or refinance into a construction loan and build
  • Your financials (personal and business, usually the last two years), a list of assets and debts, and any development track record
  • Proof of your deposit and the source of funds

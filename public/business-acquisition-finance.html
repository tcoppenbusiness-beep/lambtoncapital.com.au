How is acquisition finance assessed?

It's assessed on what's actually being bought, not a single formula.

  • Goodwill vs hard assets. A price weighted toward goodwill (the value of the business beyond its physical assets) and customer relationships is assessed more conservatively than one backed by machinery, equipment or property, because the lender has less to claim if the loan isn't repaid.
  • Vendor finance. The seller carrying part of the price can narrow the gap the main lender needs to cover.
  • Earn-out. Part of the price depends on the business hitting agreed targets, which can do the same.
  • Security. Security (assets the lender can claim if the loan isn't repaid) can be the target business, the buyer's existing assets, or both.
  • Related pages. See other business finance, or refinancing an existing loan once the acquisition has settled (the loan funds are paid and the deal completes).

What difference does vendor finance or an earn-out make?

It reduces how much the main lender has to fund, which usually improves the terms on offer.

  • Vendor loan. A vendor loan of 10–20% of the price, repaid after the main lender, is common when owner-operated businesses change hands.
  • Earn-out. It ties part of the payment to future performance, which lowers the amount needed on day one.
  • More complexity. It complicates due diligence (the checking of the business and the deal), since the lender still wants to understand the whole purchase price and how it's structured.

Example structure — illustrative

Trades business acquisition, Geelong VIC

Situation: A tradesperson buying an established business on the owner's retirement, including machinery, vehicles and a client base.

Constraint: Goodwill was a meaningful share of the price, and the buyer had few property assets of their own to offer as security.

Structure: Loan from a lender that isn't a bank, repaid over 7 years and secured by business assets, plus a 15% vendor loan that ranks behind the main loan.

Loan $980k · LVR n/a · Lender tier Non-bank · Days to settle 46

Example structure — illustrative

Franchise acquisition, Rockhampton QLD

Situation: An existing franchisee buying a second territory from a retiring operator within the same network.

Constraint: Two territories together increased the lender's exposure to a single brand.

Structure: Loan from a mid-tier bank (smaller than the big four), secured by residential property, with an earn-out covering 10% of the price over two years.

Loan $720k · LVR 65% · Lender tier Mid-tier bank · Days to settle 39

What lenders ask for

  • Financial statements and tax returns for the business being bought, most recent three years
  • Sale contract, including any vendor finance or earn-out terms
  • The buyer's financials, a personal statement of assets and debts, and identification
  • Details of the assets offered as security — business assets, property, or both

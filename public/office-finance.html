How much can I borrow to buy an office?

Usually 60–70% of the lender-ordered property valuation, set by each lender as an indicative LVR (loan-to-value ratio — the loan as a percentage of the property's value).

  • Lower than industrial. Often below what the same lender offers on an industrial unit, because offices are harder to re-let and resell when a tenant leaves.
  • Your deposit. You fund the rest, plus stamp duty and legal costs on top.
  • Owner-occupier terms. If the property is also commercial property you are buying for your own business to use, owner-occupier pricing and limits usually apply.
  • Low end. A CBD tower floor with high vacancy nearby sits at the low end.
  • Top end. A well-located suburban office near transport, with parking, can reach the top of the range.
  • Strong buyers. A buyer with strong financials and a business that clearly pays its own way can sometimes push past it.

Does it matter if the office is strata or freehold?

Yes, lenders are more cautious about strata offices than freehold ones.

  • Freehold vs strata. Freehold means you own the whole building and the land. Strata means you own one suite or floor in a building run by an owners corporation (the body that manages shared areas and charges levies).
  • Less control. You do not control the building, and a dispute or a big repair bill between owners can hit your costs.
  • Small suites. Suites under about 40 m² are hard to fund. Many lenders treat them like a parking space or storage unit, with a lower LVR or a flat decline.
  • Older towers. Offices in older towers with large levies or planned major works get a harder look.
  • Strata records. Before approving, lenders check the levy balance, the sinking fund (savings for building repairs) and any special levies announced.

How do lenders treat an office I will use myself, compared with one leased to a tenant?

An owner-occupied office is assessed mainly on your business's ability to pay the repayments; a leased one on the rent and how long the tenant is committed.

  • Leased offices. Lenders measure tenant commitment with WALE (weighted average lease expiry — the average time left on the leases). A short WALE, or a single tenant on a month-to-month arrangement, lowers the LVR.
  • Buying through an SMSF. Buying through your self-managed super fund (SMSF) and leasing the office back to your own business is allowed, but the rent must be at market rate and the lease properly documented.
  • Self-employed buyers. Without two full years of tax returns, you can look at low-doc options (assessed on fewer financial documents, such as BAS — Business Activity Statements — and an accountant's letter).

Example structure — illustrative

Suburban office suite, Box Hill VIC

Situation: An engineering consulting firm buying a 190 m² office on level 2 of a 1990s strata building, instead of renewing a lease that was rising 6% a year.

Constraint: The building had two vacant suites, and the owners corporation had announced a lift upgrade levy within the next 12 months.

Structure: Loan from a mid-tier bank (a smaller bank than the big four), secured by the suite and a first-ranking claim over the business. The lender allowed for the lift levy in its repayment test and held the LVR at 65%.

What lenders ask for

  • The contract of sale and, for strata, the owners corporation records: levy notices, minutes and the sinking fund balance
  • Your business's last two years of financials and tax returns (or low-doc evidence if applicable)
  • A lease and rent schedule if tenants are in place, including expiry dates
  • Proof of the deposit and where it came from, plus your personal assets and debts
  • The lender's own property valuation, which you pay for and which can differ from the contract price
